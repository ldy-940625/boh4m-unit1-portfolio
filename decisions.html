Better information makes better decisions possible

The café owner may never know exactly how busy a new shop would be. Before signing a lease, they can still ask a sharper question, compare a few realistic options, and check what happens after a change.

01 / Information quality

Five checks before using information

The owner wants to know if a second location could pay its way. A sales dashboard is a starting point, but its figures need five checks.

1

Timely

Last December’s sales will not describe a normal October. The report also has to arrive before the lease is signed.

2

High quality

A sales total is misleading if cancelled orders are still counted. The owner checks how the number was recorded.

3

Complete

Put rent, wages, expected orders, and setup costs together. Sales alone cannot show whether the shop will pay its bills.

4

Relevant

Foot traffic near the proposed address helps. Citywide population growth says less about that particular street.

5

Understandable

A chart should name its weeks and label its dollar amounts. Otherwise the owner may read it the wrong way.

Technology helps when managers use it carefully. The order system shows when sales peak. Staff can explain why a queue forms; the owner should use both views and keep customer details private.
02 / Problem solving

Managers turn a concern into a testable question

“We need another café” jumps straight to a solution. The owner first needs to know what the current café cannot handle: the lunch queue, seating, or both.

NOTICE

Spot the gap

The lunch queue runs past ten minutes on some school days. The owner records when it happens instead of treating every busy shift as a crisis.

CHECK

Look for causes

Staff say many delays start at the pickup shelf, not in the kitchen. Order records can help test that explanation.

ACT

Choose and review

Try a separate pickup shelf for four weeks. If waits fall without adding much cost, a second lease can wait.

03 / Decision process

Five steps from problem to result

A choice is not finished when it is announced. The owner needs a date to review it and a way to tell if it helped.

01

Define the problem

Is the problem long waits, lost orders, or too little seating? Those point to different fixes.

02

Develop and assess options

Price a second shop, longer hours, and a pickup counter. Compare likely demand and staffing as well as rent.

03

Choose a solution

If the pickup counter solves most waits at a manageable cost, choose it. Check the staffing plan before approval.

04

Put it into action

The shift supervisor sets up the counter, trains staff on handoffs, and tells customers where to collect orders.

05

Evaluate results

After four weeks, compare wait times, order errors, and costs with the old process. Keep or change the plan based on those results.

An ethical check is part of step 3

The cheapest roster might leave one person alone during the rush. The owner should reject it if it creates unsafe work or leaves customers without proper service.

04 / Group decision making

More voices can help, if the group works well

The owner, shift supervisor, cashier, and kitchen staff see different parts of the same problem. A short meeting can reveal options the owner missed.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
More knowledge: the cashier hears pickup complaints; kitchen staff know when preparation slows.More time: a meeting and follow-up delay the decision.
More options: someone may suggest a pickup counter before the owner commits to a second lease.Conflict: people may defend their preferred shifts or job roles.
Better support: staff know why the change was chosen and can flag practical snags early.Groupthink: a new employee may agree out loud while privately thinking the plan will fail.
A practical safeguard: ask everyone to write down one risk before discussion. Give one person the job of challenging the favourite idea, then record the reason for the final choice.
