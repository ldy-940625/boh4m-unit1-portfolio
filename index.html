Management fundamentals

A store can be busy and still be poorly managed.

Good managers look beyond sales. They ask how work is organized, whether customers get what they need, and what the team spends to make it happen.

Start with the organization
Illustrative case
A neighbourhood grocery store has long evening queues.

The owner is considering another checkout and, later, a second location. This simple case is used throughout the portfolio to test each management idea.

01 · The organization

People and resources working toward a goal

An organization is a structured group of people who work together to achieve a purpose. It needs roles, communication, and ways to get work done. A grocery store, a charity, and a school all count, even though their goals differ.

What “open system” means here

The store takes in food, staff time, money, equipment, and information. Employees turn these inputs into stocked shelves and customer service. Sales and complaints go back to the manager as feedback.

The store cannot plan in isolation. Supplier delays, customer demand, and competitors can change the next order or staffing schedule.

02 · Organizational performance

Three measures, three different questions

The course separates productivity, performance effectiveness, and efficiency. One good number cannot stand in for all three.

01

Productivity

Work produced, including its quantity and quality, compared with resources used.

Store check: customers served per staff hour, with order errors counted too.
02

Effectiveness

Whether the store reaches its goal.

Store check: did the evening queue become shorter without upsetting customers?
03

Efficiency

How much time, labour, and money the goal costs.

Store check: does the extra checkout save enough waiting time to justify its staffing cost?

Why managers need all three

Opening another register may serve more shoppers. If checkout mistakes also rise, productivity has a quality problem. If queues barely change, the plan is ineffective. If waits fall but wages rise far beyond the benefit, it is inefficient.

03 · Today's workplace

What changes the manager’s plan?

Globalization

Supply is connected

Imported produce offers choice, but a late shipment or higher transport cost can change prices and stock. The manager needs backup suppliers.

Diversity

People bring different needs

A mixed team may notice customer needs the owner misses. Fair shifts and clear, respectful communication help staff contribute.

Technology

Data helps when checked

Checkout records show busy hours. The manager should compare them with staff observations, fix bad data, and protect customer details.

04 · Management levels

Who makes which decision?

The course distinguishes top, middle, and first-line managers. A small store may have one person covering two levels, but the responsibilities still differ.

Top

Owner

Sets the long-term direction and decides whether expansion fits the store’s purpose and finances.

Middle

Operations manager

Turns that direction into store plans, budgets, and coordination across teams.

First-line

Shift supervisor

Guides cashiers, trains new staff, and handles the daily queue.

Workforce

Store employees

Serve shoppers and report problems the management team may not see.

The management process

Planning, organizing, leading, controlling

To test a second checkout, the owner sets a waiting-time goal, assigns staff and equipment, explains the trial, then compares results with the goal. That is the four-function cycle from Unit 1.4 in one everyday decision.
